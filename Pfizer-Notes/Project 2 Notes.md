#### Things Change Over Time

Pharmaceutical companies follow a guideline: vendor documentation should be reviewed and refreshed every **3-4 years**. This is because:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|What Changes|Why It Matters|
|**Manufacturing processes**|A supplier might reformulate a component or switch production methods|
|**Regulations**|New FDA or EU requirements get added over time|
|**Testing standards**|What was acceptable in 2009 might not meet 2024 standards|
|**Materials**|Long-term stability data reveals new information|
A certificate from 2009 doesn't guarantee the component is still made the same way in 2024. The vendor could have changed their manufacturing process five times since then—and you'd never know without updated documentation.

That's exactly what the FDA investigator was checking for.

### 🔬 Why Every Component Needs Documentation

Here's the thing about pharmaceutical manufacturing: **every single component** that goes into a drug needs documentation proving it's safe and manufactured correctly. Not just the active ingredient—everything.

Let's go back to that rubber stopper from our story. It might seem like a small thing, but think about what it does:

- It **seals** the vial containing an injectable medication

- It **touches** the drug inside, sometimes for months or years on the shelf

- Patients **inject** whatever was in contact with that stopper directly into their bodies

So that "simple" rubber stopper needs to be:

- **Free from contaminants** that could leach into the medication

- **Chemically compatible** with the drug (some drugs react with certain rubber compounds)

- **Manufactured consistently**, batch after batch

- **Traceable** back to its source if something goes wrong

The documentation proves all of this. Without it? There's no way to verify that what's going into a patient's body is safe.

### ⚠️ What Happens When Someone Gets It Wrong

FDA warning letters are public. Anyone can read them. They damage the company's reputation, delay product approvals, and can result in:

- **Import alerts** — Products blocked from entering the US market

- **Consent decrees** — Court-ordered manufacturing restrictions

- **Product recalls** — Removing products already on shelves

- **Criminal charges** — In severe cases of data falsification

Here's a number that should get your attention: in 2023, only about **50% of new drug applications were approved on first submission**. Documentation issues were among the leading causes of delays. Resubmissions took a median of **435 additional days**.

That's over a year of delay. Because of paperwork.
